In February 2026, the government told Parliament that the e-Courts Project, India's two-decade effort to digitise the country's district and subordinate courts, had made significant strides under its third phase. Phase III, approved with a budget of ₹7,210 crore in 2023, had digitised 637 crore pages of court records, processed nearly 10 crore traffic challans through virtual courts, and enabled close to four crore video hearings. The same month, the Union Budget allocated another ₹1,200 crore to sustain the momentum. The infrastructure numbers are impressive. But by the measure that matters most, that is, cases resolved and justice delivered, five crore cases remain pending.

The gap between vision and delivery is not a budget or technology problem anymore. Accelerating this transformation will require drawing on lessons from some of India's most successful large-scale digital programmes.

## [](<>)**What India's Digital Transformations Got Right**

Every successful digital transformation that India has pulled off has followed the same pattern: a clear public mandate, a dedicated government or quasi-government authority and public-spirited experts brought inside to help deliver it. Aadhaar was built under the guidance of Nandan Nilekani, one of the top technologists brought in from the private sector. UPI's open architecture was shaped in large part by iSPIRT volunteers, a non-profit technology think tank founded in 2013 to foster the Indian software product ecosystem. CoWIN's credentialing infrastructure was built by eGov Foundation, whose open-source DIVOC platform issued over two billion vaccination certificates across the world.

In each case, the government owned the mission but was clear that the civil service alone could not build it. The e-courts project has the same mandate. What is missing are the experts needed for effective delivery, that is, the product designers, data architects, and change management professionals who can turn a technical roadmap into something a litigant in Varanasi or a registry clerk in Chennai can actually use.

## The Judiciary Has Seen What Works, But Only in Glimpses

The judiciary has already seen what is possible when it leverages outside expertise. SUPACE, the Supreme Court's AI research tool, was built by a startup. E-filing defect detection tools are being developed with IIT Madras. The Madras High Court this year became the first to integrate AI for analysing case records. These collaborations have shown early success but remain exceptions.

The reason lies in how technology is currently managed inside High Courts. Each High Court has a Computer Committee of sitting judges overseeing all technology decisions. With full-time adjudication responsibilities, they are stretched to effectively play this role. A judicial officer, usually a District Judge, acts as the Central Project Coordinator, managing vendors and deployments, with more judicial officers serving as nodal contacts at the district level. These are capable, committed people trained in law, not process re-engineering, product development or systems design. Further, it is a sub-optimal use of judicial time. The functional expertise needed is lacking in the current team construct.

Phase III needs these skills on the inside. Three critical roles are missing.

Product and design professionals. Every phase of e-courts has treated adoption as something training will fix after deployment. A portal launched without actively observing how users are using the system or continuously taking feedback from it  will not get used no matter how many workshops follow. Adoption is a design challenge that requires people who start from the user and work backwards and not the other way around.

Technology architects. Phase III aims to connect courts with police, prisons, and legal aid in a single interoperable system and also keep that system relevant as technology, law, and user needs evolve. This requires architects to build on the principles of modularity and interoperability so that these tech components can be updated or replaced as needs and technology evolve quickly. It also ensures different parts of the justice system can exchange data without being locked into a single vendor. Judicial data is uniquely high-stakes. A misconfigured access control does not produce a failed transaction; it can mean a bail order not reaching a prison on time or an acquitted person's records being seen by the wrong people. Getting the architecture and data standards right from the start prevents those failures.

Change management professionals. Asking judges, court staff, lawyers, and litigants to simultaneously shift long-standing habits is an organisational challenge, not just a technical one. It requires its own experts who are working on making that change happen and are accountable for the outcomes. Change management professionals map existing workflows to find where the friction is, identify which user groups are resisting and why, and redesign the rollout around those findings. They engage with users frequently and use data to understand gaps and opportunities for continuous improvement. Their full-time mandate is to understand why the system is not being used and to change that.

## A Proven Model, and the Gaps to Watch

The model for how to bring in this talent already exists. The National Health Authority, NeGD, and many central ministries hire specialists and experts externally. NITI Aayog runs a Young Professionals programme for early-career policy professionals. State governments run fellowship programmes to bring in professionals for specific mandates. We have seen procurement of services via contracts in the case of the Passport Seva Kendras that are managed by TCS. Across the executive, hundreds of outside experts work within government on fixed-term contracts. There is no shortage of proven models.

The objection most commonly raised to engaging experts is the risk of losing  judicial independence and the security of data. But independence applies to adjudication, not administration. A product designer improving an e-filing workflow has no bearing on how a judge decides a case. Further data security can be easily ensured by making sure the data remains with the courts, creating contractual accountability and providing access, if any, only with authorisation. Done right, specialist hiring is not a risk.

The litigant in Varanasi and the registry clerk in Chennai are not waiting for a better algorithm. They are waiting for someone who designed the system with them in mind, someone who made sure the data flowing through it was safe, and someone who stayed long enough to bring about an irreversible change. That is what experts can do. And that is the missing link here.
