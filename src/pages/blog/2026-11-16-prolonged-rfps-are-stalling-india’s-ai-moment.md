A Director in the Planning Department of an Indian state recently sat through a three-hour pitch from an AI vendor promising to “revolutionise last-mile service delivery.” The vendor’s deck was polished. The demo was compelling. The Director was genuinely interested. Six months later, nothing had moved. The RFP remained entangled in a maze of administrative processes, the procurement committee was conducting due diligence, and the effort eventually dissolved after elections, when the new government chose to begin again from scratch.

The executors are willing. The processes are what need fixing. This scene plays out simultaneously across scores of departments in Indian governance, and addressing it requires looking squarely at the procedural architecture that governs AI adoption.

## The Scale of What Is Being Left on the Table

The private sector has moved decisively. Global corporate investment in AI reached an estimated $252 billion in 2024, up from $92 billion in 2020, a near-fourfold rise in four years. McKinsey estimates that AI could add between $2.6 trillion and $4.4 trillion annually to the global economy through productivity gains, smarter operations, and new product development. Goldman Sachs projects that generative AI alone could raise global GDP by 7% over the next decade.

The gains are visible and measurable. Organisations that have embedded AI into core operations report meaningful improvements in operational efficiency and outcomes. In the private sector- healthcare diagnostics, manufacturing defect detection, credit underwriting, and customer service, AI has become core infrastructure. This transformation does carry a shadow: automation-led displacement without a commensurate safety net would be catastrophic, and an effective welfare state is imperative to infuse inclusivity into growth.

 

At precisely the moment when AI is generating enormous economic value in the private sector and creating social disruption that demands a more capable state, Indian governments are far from equipped to harness it. The return on investment of AI adoption in governance is, by most serious estimates, among the highest of any sector. This is backed by theoretical analysis and practical examples, and the developing world stands most to gain.

India does have success stories. The CoWIN platform, built and deployed in roughly 90 days at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, is the most cited. At its peak it was processing millions of vaccination bookings per day, generating real-time slot availability data, and dynamically allocating doses across the country. The underlying architecture used predictive analytics to model demand surges and trigger supply reallocation across geographies. It worked because it bypassed the normal procurement process entirely, classified as a national emergency response, built on a war footing with a small empowered team and zero overhead.

CoWIN is genuinely impressive but equally exceptional. It succeeded as it was built beyond the normal system, running on crisis authority. India has demonstrated that it can build excellent AI-enabled systems. The remaining challenge is institutionalizing procurement and deployment through conventional administrative channels.

## Problem One: The Discovery Gap

Before a government can adopt AI, it needs to know what to adopt. In practice, this is where most efforts stall.

State-level decision-makers like Principal Secretaries, Mission Directors, departmental Commissioners are buried in vendors. Every AI startup, every large IT integrator, and every consulting firm with a freshly renamed “AI practice” is in their waiting room. The pitches are structurally identical: powerful claims, polished demonstrations on curated data, and a scope of work that invariably expands once the contract is signed. The official receiving these pitches has no reliable framework for distinguishing a transformative intervention from an expensive disappointment. The fear of the latter looms large in the bureaucracy.

What is missing is a systematic approach to solution discovery: a mechanism for identifying which AI use cases are actually ready for deployment in Indian public sector conditions, which have demonstrated measurable impact in comparable geographies, and which align with the specific data, administrative, and last-mile constraints of a given state.

Two predictable failure modes result. The first is paralysis: officials, unable to evaluate claims, default to inaction. The second is reinvention: states with the appetite to act spend 18–24 months building from scratch what already exists elsewhere. India lacks a robust institutional mechanism allowing state governments to rapidly identify proven AI interventions and assess their transferability. Until that infrastructure is built, each state will continue to treat a solved problem as an open question.

## Problem Two: The Procurement Trap

Even states that correctly identify what they want to adopt routinely fail to acquire it. Indian government procurement was designed for a world of physical goods and fixed deliverables. The L1 and Quality and Cost Based (QCB) systems are rational responses to a genuine problem: preventing corruption in procurement of standardised products and services. They work reasonably well for cement, stationery, or basic software licences. Applied to AI, the fit breaks down fundamentally.

AI systems are highly iterative. A machine learning model procured in January 2025 will be technically obsolete in ways difficult to anticipate by December of the same year. The typical government RFP process in India takes six to twelve months from initiation to contract award. In the AI sector, that is geological time. A contract that defines deliverables at the point of signing, with limited provision for adaptive iteration, will bind the government to an outdated specification. Even change requests, under conventional procurement pathways, are limited and cumbersome.

The risk-aversion embedded in the current system also operates at the individual official level. An officer who approves a flexible, outcome-based contract for an AI system that subsequently underperforms faces genuine liability under procurement audit. An officer who follows the process to the letter and procures something that delivers poor results faces no accountability at all. The incentive structure systematically rewards procedural compliance over impact.

## What a Better System Looks Like

Several jurisdictions have already solved this. Their models require administrative will more than technical innovation.

The United Kingdom’s Digital Outcomes and Specialists framework allows government departments to procure digital services, including AI, through a pre-qualified supplier panel on an outcomes basis. Contracts specify what the system must achieve, not how it must be built. Payment milestones are tied to demonstrated performance against pre-agreed KPIs, rather than to delivery of technical specifications. The framework is updated regularly, meaning the supplier ecosystem reflects current market capabilities.

Singapore’s GovTech has institutionalised technology sandboxes- controlled environments in which vendors can pilot AI systems using real government data under limited conditions, generating evidence before full procurement.⁵ This shifts the evaluation burden from theoretical RFP scoring to empirical performance, and substantially reduces the information asymmetry that favours incumbent vendors.

For Indian states, the practical implication is a shift in where accountability is concentrated. The current model front-loads accountability into the procurement process- elaborate documentation, multi-stage approvals, committee oversight and then effectively releases or shifts accountability at the point of contract award. A better model compresses the pre-procurement phase and transfers accountability to the delivery phase, where performance bonds, escrow-based milestone payments, and mandatory KPI audits keep vendor incentives aligned throughout implementation.

States can also create specialised technical panels: standing committees of domain experts and technologists (including those from the private sector) empowered to evaluate AI proposals on capability dimensions that standard processes cannot assess. Defence procurement in India has long operated specialist technical evaluation mechanisms. The same approach applies directly to AI for public services.

## A Playbook for Forward-Thinking States

Indian state governments are, in many respects, already solving each other’s problems- just without any organised mechanism to share what they learn. Officials in one state secretariat have limited ways of knowing what a comparable department three states away spent two years learning the hard way. The reinvention is wasteful, and it slows the entire system at a moment when speed matters.

An RFP process that takes eight months to complete functions as a structural disqualifier in the context of AI. By the time a contract is awarded under standard timelines, the technical assumptions embedded in the original tender document are likely obsolete and the vendor landscape has shifted. Slow procurement actively produces worse outcomes by locking governments into specifications written before the technology had fully matured.

India’s states do not need to design this from first principles. Corrective models exist. The evidence base is accumulating. The step that remains is treating AI procurement as a distinct administrative challenge requiring distinct administrative responses, rather than channeling it through procurement architecture built for office supplies and construction contracts. The countries and states that have moved are less attached to process uniformity as an end in itself.

The cost of delay is a continued divergence between what governments promise and what citizens receive- at a moment when that gap is becoming electorally, fiscally, and morally harder to sustain.
